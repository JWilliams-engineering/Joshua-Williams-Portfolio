Dual-Axis Solar Tracking System

An Arduino-controlled dual-axis sun-tracking system designed to quantify and improve photovoltaic energy yield on constrained north-facing rooftop installations - developed as independent Honors Research and presented at the Penn State Hazleton Spring 2025 Research Fair.

🏆  Research Award - 1st Place
"South-Facing Solar Tracker Performance on a North-Facing Roof"
Penn State Hazleton Annual Research Symposium  ·  Spring 2025  ·  First Place - Undergraduate Research Category

Problem Statement

North-facing roof-mounted photovoltaic panels in the northern hemisphere operate at a fixed, suboptimal angle relative to the sun's daily arc. As solar azimuth and elevation shift throughout the day, a stationary panel can forfeit 25-40% of potential energy capture compared to an actively tracked equivalent. The engineering challenge was to design and build a dual-axis tracking system capable of dynamically orienting a PV panel toward peak irradiance, then quantify the real energy yield improvement through physical testing and measurement.

My Role

This was a solo Honors Research project. I was solely responsible for every stage of design, fabrication, programming, and analysis:

  • Full mechanical frame design and fabrication using CAD and additive manufacturing
  • Dual-axis gimbal geometry and actuator selection for azimuth and elevation control
  • Arduino firmware development for closed-loop sun-position tracking
  • Sensor integration, wiring, and power system design
  • Data collection, performance analysis, and research presentation

Engineering Approach

The system uses an Arduino microcontroller to compute solar azimuth and elevation in real time from GPS-derived coordinates and a real-time clock (RTC) module, then drives two servo-actuated axes to orient the PV panel toward calculated peak irradiance. Unlike light-dependent resistor (LDR) sensor approaches - which are susceptible to cloud-cover noise and local shading - the algorithm-based approach provides predictable, smooth tracking behaviour throughout the day regardless of transient irradiance conditions.

The structural frame was designed in Fusion 360 and fabricated using 3D-printed components, a fabrication method applied professionally at Insteel Wire Products to reduce component costs by up to 97%. This allowed for rapid iteration of the gimbal geometry without tooling cost, while keeping the overall system compact and mountable on a standard rooftop parapet.

System Breakdown

Subsystem Overview

Each subsystem was scoped, designed, and validated independently before integration into the full dual-axis assembly.

01

Mechanical Structure

  • Frame designed in Fusion 360 and fabricated via 3D printing for rapid iteration
  • Dual-axis gimbal geometry allows independent azimuth and elevation adjustment
  • 3D-printed components replaced off-the-shelf hardware, reducing per-unit cost significantly
  • Compact footprint designed for rooftop parapet mounting without permanent attachment
  • Gen 2 redesign in progress with improved joint stiffness and refined panel interface
02

Actuation System

  • Two servo motors drive azimuth and elevation axes independently
  • Servo torque rated to overcome panel aerodynamic and gravitational loading at design angles
  • Direct-drive configuration with no gearbox, minimising mechanical complexity and backlash
  • Servo pulse-width commands generated in firmware based on calculated sun position
  • Axis travel limits enforced in software to prevent over-rotation and cable strain
03

Braking & Position Hold

  • Servo motors hold commanded position actively - no passive brake required
  • Panel position maintained at last valid sun-position calculation between update intervals
  • Dead-band implemented in firmware to suppress unnecessary micro-actuation in stable conditions
  • On power loss, servos de-energise and panel returns to neutral resting position under gravity
  • Gen 2 design evaluates locking hinge geometry to reduce continuous servo hold current
04

Control System

  • Arduino microcontroller chosen for accessible I/O, C++ programmability, and prototyping speed
  • Solar azimuth and elevation computed in firmware from GPS coordinates and RTC timestamp
  • Algorithm-based tracking eliminates LDR sensor dependency and cloud-cover noise
  • Control loop executes every 60 s - matched to practical solar angular movement resolution
  • Serial output logs computed sun position and servo commands for post-run data analysis
05

Electrical / Power System

  • Powered from a regulated 5 V USB supply during lab testing with a battery pack for outdoor deployment
  • Arduino supplies servo PWM signals directly with servo power supplied via separate 5 V rail
  • GPS module and RTC integrated on shared I2C/Serial bus with Arduino Uno
  • Current draw characterised across tracking range to inform battery sizing for Gen 2
  • Wiring routed along frame members and secured to prevent interference with moving axes

Engineering Tradeoffs

An algorithm-driven sun-position approach was chosen over a common light-dependent resistor (LDR) feedback system. While LDR-based trackers are simpler to implement, they are inherently noisy under variable cloud cover and fail to distinguish diffuse irradiance from direct beam radiation. The GPS/RTC computational approach produces smooth, predictable tracking behaviour with no sensor drift - at the cost of slightly higher firmware complexity and a dependence on accurate time synchronisation.

Using 3D-printed structural components enabled rapid design iteration and dramatically reduced fabrication cost - a strategy validated professionally during the internship at Insteel Wire Products, where additive manufacturing reduced component costs by up to 97%. For a field-hardened Gen 2 deployment, critical load-bearing joints would be transitioned to machined aluminum to improve long-term fatigue resistance in outdoor conditions.

Technical Learnings

This project demonstrated how closely mechanical design, embedded control, and power system constraints interact in a real energy engineering application. Frame geometry decisions directly influenced servo torque requirements, which drove power budget estimates, which sized the portable battery system - each subsystem decision tightened the constraints on the next.

Presenting the research at the Penn State Hazleton Symposium and placing first reinforced the importance of communicating technical work clearly to a mixed engineering and non-engineering audience. The Gen 2 project builds directly on documented shortcomings from Gen 1, including improved joint rigidity, outdoor weatherproofing, and a structured data logging system for long-term energy yield measurement.
